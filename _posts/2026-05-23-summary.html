Student team creates simpler method for programming artificial muscles

rss · Phys.org · May 22, 19:20

EN

An interdisciplinary student team at the University of Waterloo developed a tissue-like hydrogel that allows artificial muscles in soft robots to be programmed more simply, enabling them to bend and change shape in response to triggers such as pH or salinity. The hydrogel combines mechanical toughness with high conductivity, making it easier to control the muscles without complex actuators.

This advancement could lower the complexity and cost of soft robotic systems, making them more accessible for applications in healthcare, wearable devices, and environmental monitoring. It also demonstrates how student-driven research can yield practical materials that bridge biology and engineering.

The hydrogel mimics the modulus of biological tissue, providing a tissue-like mechanical property while remaining highly conductive. Programming is achieved by varying input mechanical training strain or adjusting the density of coordination bonds, allowing precise deformation control. The method simplifies fabrication compared to previous approaches that required complex electrical or thermal stimuli.

Hydrogels are water‐rich, Jell‐O‐like materials that can mimic the mechanical and chemical behavior of biological tissues, as demonstrated by researchers at the University of Colorado Boulder who created a light‐controlled, stretchable platform. Recent work published in Nature Communications shows that a synthetic template‐directed assembly can produce highly conductive and mechanically tough hydrogels with tissue‐like modulus, suitable for interfacing with living tissue. Artificial muscles, or muscle‐like actuators, are materials that can reversibly contract, expand, or change stiffness, and they rely on such smart hydrogels to be programmed for specific motions. The University of Waterloo student team’s hydrogel therefore bridges the gap between soft robotics and tissue engineering, offering a simpler route to program these actuators.

Under the CHIPS Act, the US is investing heavily to triple its semiconductor manufacturing capacity by 2032, with companies like Intel committing over $100 billion to domestic production facilities.
